Case study

How I Made ProductIQ

I made ProductIQ from the Amazon product research code I had already been working on. The scripts worked, but I had too many separate versions and still had to do too much of the process myself. I wanted one app where I could upload or paste a list of products, research them, deal with problems without losing everything, and export the results when I was done.

The actual problem

I already had code for this, but I was still doing too much by hand.

ProductIQ did not start because I wanted another dashboard. It started because I kept ending up in the same situation: a spreadsheet full of products, a pile of Amazon tabs, and the same information being copied one listing at a time.

One product is easy. Twenty-five is annoying. A few hundred turns into hours of opening pages, making sure I am looking at the right product, copying titles and descriptions, saving image links, checking dimensions, prices, sellers, ratings, and whatever else I need, then putting all of it back into the right spreadsheet row. The work is repetitive enough that mistakes are almost built into the process.

I had already written Amazon scraping scripts because I did not want to keep doing that manually. Those scripts were useful and they worked. Over time I kept adding more to them: spreadsheet processing, product search, descriptions, images, retries, checkpoints, and CAPTCHA handling. The code kept getting better, but the way I had to use it did not.

Eventually I had several versions that solved slightly different parts of the same problem. I knew which one I needed and what to change before I ran it, but that is not the same thing as having one finished tool. ProductIQ is me taking the useful code I already had and building the missing workflow around it.

What I kept

The real Amazon extraction

The scraper is still the point. I was not interested in replacing it with generated sample information just so the interface looked finished.

What I changed

Everything around the scraper

Input, queues, progress, recovery, saved results, retries, exports, and a UI that does not require me to think about which Python file I need first.

Using the product information I already have

I did not want to re-enter a product list just to research it.

Most of the time I already have a product list before I open the app. It might be a CSV, an Excel file, a supplier sheet, or something I exported from somewhere else. Sometimes I have an ASIN. Sometimes I have an Amazon URL. Sometimes I only have the product name. I built ProductIQ around those situations instead of deciding there was one perfect way every product had to be entered.

For a few products I can paste one ASIN, URL, or product name per line. For a larger list I can upload CSV or XLSX and map the columns that already exist. That was important to me because the entire point is to avoid re-entering product information I already have.

What I wanted ProductIQ to find

I needed it to pull the information I was actually looking for.

The fields I wanted from the beginning were the fields I kept having to look up myself: title, bullet points, description, images, dimensions, weight, price, availability, seller information, ratings, review count, model details, manufacturer information, category paths, and other technical details when they are present.

Amazon does not lay every category out the same way, so the parser cannot depend on one exact selector for every field. ProductIQ checks several common locations, cleans up the values it finds, and leaves fields empty when the page does not actually provide them.

I do not want the app filling a blank field with something that only looks plausible. If ProductIQ cannot verify a value from the listing, I would rather know that the row needs review. The research is only useful if I can trust where the information came from.

Result states

Complete does not mean “every possible field exists.”

It means ProductIQ got a usable listing result. “Needs review” is there for partial or questionable results. “Error” is there when the request could not produce a usable product record.

Working with Amazon

Amazon was also the part that caused the most problems.

This is where building the app became more than moving a few Python functions into Flask. Amazon can return different page layouts, missing fields, unavailable listings, redirects, search results that are not confident enough, or a CAPTCHA instead of the page ProductIQ asked for.

I cannot control those responses. What I can control is what ProductIQ does next. I wanted a failed page to be one product that needs attention, not the thing that destroys an entire batch.

The important part

One failed product should not erase everything that already worked.

Completed results stay available while the next product is being researched. Incomplete products can be requeued. The batch can stop without pretending everything before the stop also failed.

CAPTCHA handling

I wanted the CAPTCHA handling to work the same way it did in my original code.

The original code did not automatically solve Amazon CAPTCHAs. When Amazon asked for human verification, the script recognized it, paused, let me solve the challenge, and continued. That is the behavior I wanted in ProductIQ too.

The hosted version has to do more work to make that happen because the CAPTCHA is connected to the Amazon session that received it. Cookies, headers, browser identity, the challenge form, and the image request all have to stay together. If the app treats the CAPTCHA image like an unrelated file, it may not load or Amazon may reject the answer.

ProductIQ pauses the active research job, keeps the current session, shows the challenge, accepts the user's answer, and then continues if Amazon accepts it. The point is not to bypass the CAPTCHA. The point is to stop a CAPTCHA from turning into a lost batch.

Why this matters

Automation still needs a human sometimes.

I am fine with that. The app should automate the repetitive work and make the unavoidable manual interruption as painless as possible.

Why I kept the 25-product limit

I could make the input accept hundreds of rows. That does not mean the free hosted version should attack Amazon with hundreds of requests at once.

The current hosted version intentionally limits a batch to twenty-five products. The processing is sequential and slowed down on purpose. Free hosting has limited resources, and Amazon is more likely to challenge or block a session when requests are too aggressive. A smaller batch is less exciting on a feature list, but it is more useful if it actually finishes.

The limit is not part of the underlying idea of ProductIQ. It is a practical choice for this deployment. The queue, retry system, and exports are designed so batches can be handled in a controlled way instead of pretending there is no cost to unlimited automation.

Sample data

I wanted the sample products to work without downloading a file first.

The repository includes sample CSV and XLSX files because those are useful when someone is looking at the source or wants to test the upload workflow. But I also wanted the dashboard itself to have a sample-data button.

Requiring someone to leave ProductIQ, go to GitHub, download a sample file, come back to ProductIQ, and upload the file they just downloaded is unnecessary. The dashboard now loads the same real sample products directly into the queue. The files are still included so both workflows can be tested.

Why real samples

The sample rows should be actual Amazon listings.

A fake ASIN is not useful for an application whose main job is researching Amazon. The included sample set uses real product pages so the sample can exercise the real research path.

The design

I wanted the design to look finished without getting in the way.

The interface went through a lot of visual changes because the branding needed to work on dark backgrounds, the logo assets needed cleaned up, the spacing needed to hold up on a phone, and the results needed to stay readable when there was a lot of information on the page. I ended up using the navy, purple, and blue from the ProductIQ logo with a darker gradient background and lighter glass-like surfaces.

I also consolidated the logo CSS and standardized the asset names because repeated design revisions had left several slightly different logo files and overlapping style rules in the project. That kind of cleanup is not visible as a feature, but it makes the app much easier to keep changing without breaking something two screens away.

What is next

There is still more I want to add later.

The enhanced version I have planned goes beyond Amazon extraction. I want competitor research, delivered-price comparisons, pricing and margin tools, supplier sourcing, SEO and listing content, audience ideas, related products, bundle ideas, category suggestions, and exports for different selling platforms.

I am not calling those finished just because I have planned them. The main thing I care about first is making the original product research dependable. If the application cannot reliably take a product list, research the listings, survive normal failures, and give me clean results back, then adding ten more sections does not fix the actual problem.

Competitor research

Find the same or genuinely comparable products across additional marketplaces and explain why the match is considered exact, probable, similar, or only an alternative.

Pricing tools

Use cost, fees, and competitor information to calculate break-even points, suggested prices, sale scenarios, margins, and advertising limits.

Supplier research

Compare supplier cost, shipping, MOQ, delivery estimates, processing time, ratings, policies, and risk flags without making me build the comparison by hand.

Listing content

Turn verified product data into editable titles, bullets, descriptions, metadata, keywords, FAQs, and platform-specific content without losing the original researched values.

What I learned

Getting the scraper to work was only part of finishing the app.

When I first started writing the Amazon scripts, most of my attention was on extraction. Can I find the product? Can I pull the description? Can I get the images? Can I write everything into the spreadsheet? Those were the right questions at the time.

Building ProductIQ made the questions bigger. What happens when product seven fails? What happens when Amazon returns a CAPTCHA? What if the page has a title but no price? How do I keep the successful products? How does the user know what still needs attention? How do I get the data back out without making the application another dead end?

That is the difference between having code that works and having a tool I actually want to use. ProductIQ is still built around the same Amazon research I started with. The work now is making everything around that research dependable enough that I do not have to think about the code every time I need the result.
